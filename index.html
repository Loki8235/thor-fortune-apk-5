How the peg pyramid works

The board is built as a triangle of offset pegs, with each row one peg wider than the row above it. A ball released from the top has an even chance of moving left or right at every peg, and that same split repeats until the ball reaches the bottom. The result is one of the pockets arranged along the base, with one more pocket than there are rows.

Because each step is a simple fifty-fifty choice, most balls gather around the middle and fewer reach the outer edges. The board behaves like a Galton board, and the pattern of results follows a binomial curve. In practical terms, common pockets pay less and rare outer pockets pay more, which is why the payout table looks wider on larger boards.

Multipliers, odds, and the house margin

Each pocket multiplier is shaped by the chance of reaching it, with a small house margin built in. If you multiply the payout by its probability, the total sits a little below one, and that gap is the operator's edge. Most versions of the game sit in the 97% to 99% return range, depending on the studio and the settings used.

A good reality check is simple: no row count or risk level changes the long-run return in your favour. The settings only move variance around, which means they change how bumpy the journey is rather than the average result.

Six words worth knowing

Pocket
The slot at the bottom of the board that holds the final multiplier.
Rows
The number of peg lines a ball crosses; more rows create more pockets and tighter odds.
Risk level
A preset that stretches or compresses the payout table without changing the long-run return much.
House margin
The small difference between true odds and the paid odds that supports the operator.
Provably fair
A system of published hashes that lets players verify the round result later.
Variance
The amount of swing around the average result, which is what the risk setting mainly changes.

On licensed sites, yes. The route of each ball comes from an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you check that the path was fixed before the drop.

Do more rows make the game pay better?

No. More rows add pockets and widen the odds curve, so the edge prizes become larger while their chance drops. The overall return stays about the same across board sizes.

Which risk level is the smart choice?

None is better in mathematical terms. Low risk gives smoother results, while high risk moves more value to the outer pockets. The right choice depends on your budget and tolerance for swings.
